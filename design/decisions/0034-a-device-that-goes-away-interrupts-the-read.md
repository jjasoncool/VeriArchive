# A device that goes away interrupts the read; it never makes entries unreadable

§A6 makes a run whose device disappeared `INCOMPLETE`: it claims nothing and is resumed (§B11.4). The engines told a device that went away only by the error of the read that failed: five Win32 errors and four `errno` values were `DeviceRemoved` (§B9), and every other error was the entry's own, `Unreadable` (decision 0010). A device that goes away while it is read rarely says so in its errors. On Linux the files of a USB device pulled out fail with `EIO`, the error of a bad sector; on Windows the read in flight fails with an I/O error or a timeout, and the files opened after it are "not found". A FULL verification of the operator's archive on a USB device (about 18 hours for 10 TB) whose cable came loose would then end `FAILED`, with every file after that point `Unreadable(media)` or `Unreadable(other)`: a result that stays in the history, claims damage the device does not have, and cannot be resumed, so the next verification starts again from zero. A registration recorded those files as unreadable at registration, without hashes, in the archive's baseline.

## Decision

1. **A read error is the entry's only while the volume is still attached.** When a FULL verification, a registration or a relocation cannot read an entry (a file's opening or read, a folder's listing, a link's target, the root's listing) with an error that does not by itself name a device that went away (point 3), it looks up the volume at its root again, as it did when it began (`IMediaEnumerator::volume_for_path`). The volume is still attached when the lookup finds the same volume: the same filesystem id (`filesystem_uuid`, `filesystem_uuid_short`, and on Windows `volume_guid`) and the same devices (each one's model, serial and WWN, in order; `same_volume`). Then the error is the entry's: `Unreadable(permission|media|other)` as before (decision 0010), unless it is one of ten I/O errors in a row (point 6). Otherwise the volume is gone: the lookup fails, or finds another volume, or this one without its devices or their names, as the platform reports a volume whose device has gone. Once gone, it stays gone for the rest of the operation. The label, the capacities and the rotational flag are left out: they may read otherwise while the volume stays attached.
2. **A volume that is gone interrupts the operation**, as a cancel does, and the entry whose read failed is not classified: it is neither reported nor recorded unreadable nor checkpointed, and the resume reads it again. A FULL verification ends `INCOMPLETE`, with what it verified before checkpointed (R-104), and says so: "the device that holds the archive went away while it was read (unplugged, or no longer answering); attach it again, then resume this run". A registration stops (`REGISTRATION_INCOMPLETE`) without recording a revision, its hashed files checkpointed, as when its device was reported removed. A relocation's attempt is interrupted, never a carrier that differs (decision 0026), and is recorded `INCOMPLETE` as every interrupted attempt is. An I/O error is classified only once a later read succeeds (point 6), so one a moment before the removal reached the volume is not classified either. An entry whose error is of another kind, classified before the volume could be seen gone, stays in the interrupted run's items, which claim nothing for the archive, and its resume reads that entry again.
3. **More errors name a device that went away by themselves** (§B9 `DeviceRemoved`), without a lookup. Windows: also `ERROR_MEDIA_CHANGED`, `ERROR_NO_MEDIA_IN_DRIVE`, `ERROR_UNRECOGNIZED_VOLUME` and `ERROR_FILE_INVALID` (the volume was dismounted under the open file), beside `ERROR_NOT_READY`, `ERROR_DEVICE_NOT_CONNECTED`, `ERROR_DEV_NOT_EXIST`, `ERROR_DEVICE_REMOVED` and `ERROR_NO_SUCH_DEVICE`. Linux: also `ENOLINK` (the link to the device was cut) and `ENOTCONN` (the FUSE program that served the mount has ended, as ntfs-3g does after its device went away), beside `ENODEV`, `ENXIO`, `ENOMEDIUM` and `ESHUTDOWN`. I/O errors (Linux `EIO`; Windows `ERROR_IO_DEVICE`, `ERROR_GEN_FAILURE`, `ERROR_CRC`) stay media errors and timeouts stay timeouts: a bad sector gives them too, and point 1 tells the two apart.
4. **`Unreadable(removed)` (§A6) is never produced.** A device that went away, or stopped answering (point 6), makes no item: decision 0010 already left `removed` out of the reasons recorded at registration, and points 2 and 6 leave it out of verification.
5. **Where.** `VolumeWatch` (`core/engine/volume_watch.h`) holds the volume an operation began with, looks it up again, and counts the I/O errors in a row (point 6); it is told of every read of the operation and asked before an entry that cannot be read is classified: by the scanner (`ScanOptions::answered`, `ScanOptions::must_stop`, which `VolumeWatch::watch_scan` sets) for each folder listed or link target read, and before it records a folder or a link as unreadable; by the comparison (verification and relocation, `ComparisonInput::watch`) for each file read; by registration in its own hash step. The volume a verification by archive uuid found among the attached volumes is looked up at the root found there, as a verification by root looks up its root.
6. **A device that stops answering interrupts the operation too** (operator, 2026-10-27). A USB enclosure that stops answering while the computer still lists its volume leaves the volume found at its root, so point 1 cannot tell it from damage: every read fails with an I/O error, as a damaged area does. Ten reads in a row that fail with an I/O error (`IoErrorKind::MediaError`, such as `EIO`, `ERROR_IO_DEVICE` or `ERROR_CRC`, or `IoErrorKind::Timeout`), with no read between them that succeeds (a file read to its end, a folder listed, a link's target read), while the volume stays attached, are taken for a device that stopped answering (`io_errors_that_stop`). An error of another kind (permission, not found, any other) neither counts nor ends such a run: it says nothing of whether the device answers. The count runs through the operation's scan and its reading of files alike; an entry below a folder that cannot be listed is not read, and does not count. The tenth error interrupts the operation as a volume that is gone does (point 2): a FULL verification ends `INCOMPLETE`, a registration stops (`REGISTRATION_INCOMPLETE`), a relocation's attempt is interrupted, and the entries whose reads failed are not classified: the verification and the relocation hold each file's I/O error back until a later read succeeds, or until every file was read, and only then is it the entry's `Unreadable(media|other)`; a registration, which records nothing of an interrupted attempt, marks it at once, and so does the scan for a folder or a link, as a scan that stops keeps nothing (a folder so marked just before the files whose reads complete the ten stays among the interrupted run's items, which claim nothing for the archive). Each says in plain words what happened and what to do. The verification: "the device that holds the archive stopped answering while it was read: 10 entries in a row could not be read; unplug it and attach it again, then resume this run; if it stops again at the same place, the device may be damaged". The registration: "the device stopped answering during registration: 10 entries in a row could not be read; unplug it and attach it again; continue with `register <root_path> --resume`; if it stops again at the same place, the device may be damaged". The relocation: "relocation was interrupted: the device of the new carrier stopped answering (10 entries in a row could not be read); unplug it and attach it again, then continue it with `archive relocate <uuid> <root_path> --resume`; if it stops again at the same place, the device may be damaged". The cost the operator accepted: damage that spans ten or more entries in a row also stops the operation instead of failing it, and its resume reads those entries again and stops at the same place, which the words name.

## Why

The operator's priority is verification, and the next step is an 18-hour FULL verification of an archive on a USB device: a loose cable must cost a resume, not a false `FAILED` in the history and a new run from zero. The error of a read cannot tell a device that went away from a bad sector on every platform and bus, while the volume can be looked up again at once, through the same call that found it, and a device that went away takes its volume, or the names of its devices, with it. The lookup costs a few calls of the platform, made only after a read failed.

A device that hangs while its volume stays listed is the same loose cable, or a tired enclosure, seen from the other side: the operator would rather resume after attaching it again than have the run end `FAILED` with every later file unreadable (point 6). Ten is the threshold: a damaged area of a disk often spans a few small files in a row, which a smaller number (three) would stop on and never report, while a device that stopped answering fails every read from then on, and each failed read of a hanging USB bridge may take a timeout of up to a minute before it returns, so a larger number (a hundred) would keep the operator waiting long before the run stops. The I/O errors are held back rather than classified as they come, so that an interrupted run lists none of the entries the device took with it.

Rejected alternatives:

- Taking `EIO` (and Windows' I/O errors) for a device that went away: a bad sector, the damage a FULL verification exists to find, would interrupt the run instead, at every resume again, and would never be reported.
- Telling a removal in the Linux platform layer from the file's device number (`st_dev`, `/sys/dev/block`): btrfs and FUSE report device numbers that name no block device, so every read error there would read as a removal; the volume lookup already knows how a mount leads to its devices.
- Reading the entry again before classifying it: a bad sector on a USB device costs seconds or minutes per read, which a second read doubles, and a device that went away is told faster by its volume.
- Waiting a moment before the lookup, so that a removal that has not yet reached the volume is seen: every unreadable file would wait, also every file the account may not read; an entry classified in that moment is read again by the resume (point 2).
- Comparing the whole volume as the lookup gives it: its label, capacities and rotational flag may read otherwise while it stays attached, and a run would then stop for nothing.
- Counting every error but a permission or a not found one toward a device that stopped answering (point 6): an error the platform names no further (`Other`), such as that of a cloud file whose content is not on the disk, would then stop a registration of a folder that holds ten of them, again at every resume.
- A time without an answer instead of a number of reads: how long a hanging device takes to fail a read differs by bus, bridge and driver, and a slow, healthy USB disk would stop for nothing.

## Consequences

- A FULL verification, a registration or a relocation whose device goes away, also with errors that do not name a removal, is resumed once the device is attached again, without reading again what it had verified, hashed or matched. Its run is `INCOMPLETE`, never `FAILED` for that reason, and a registration records no file unreadable for it.
- A volume that stays attached while every read fails (a USB bridge that hangs, its device still listed) is no removal, but ten I/O errors in a row make it a device that stopped answering (point 6): the run is `INCOMPLETE` as for a removal, and says to unplug the device, attach it again and resume. Fewer I/O errors in a row are the entries' own, `Unreadable(media)` or `Unreadable(other)` (a timeout), as before, also the last ones of a run after which no file is left to read.
- Damage that spans ten or more entries in a row stops the run at that place at every resume; the words say that the device may be damaged.
- A read error costs one more lookup of the volume while the volume stays attached; nothing changes while every read succeeds.
- No change to the manifests, the database or the golden test.
- Tests: with the fakes, a device that goes away, its reads failing with I/O errors and its volume no longer found, at the first file, halfway through a later file and while the folders are scanned, in a verification, a registration and a relocation, each resumed once it is attached again; a device that stops answering, its volume still found, at a file's opening, halfway through a file's read and while the folders are listed, in the same three operations, each resumed; nine I/O errors in a row before a file that reads, or at the end, stay the entries' own, two runs of five with a file read between them too, and so do five folders that cannot be listed, a folder listed, and five damaged files; a permission error neither counts nor ends a run; how the watch tells each error (`VolumeWatch::failed`); whether a volume is the same (`same_volume`); the errors each platform names a removal (built on each OS). On hardware, a FULL verification through a USB bridge with the device pulled out once stays an acceptance item on both OSes.

## Operator decisions (2026-10-27)

1. **A device that stops answering while the computer still lists its volume** (a USB enclosure that hangs): when several entries in a row cannot be read because of I/O errors, not permission or not-found errors, the verification, and a registration or a relocation reading the media, stops as interrupted, recorded `INCOMPLETE` and resumable, and says in plain words that the device stopped answering: attach it again and resume; if it stops again at the same place, the device may be damaged. The threshold is ten entries in a row with no read between them that succeeds (point 6). The cost the operator accepted: a device whose damage spans that many entries in a row also stops instead of failing; a resume reads those entries again.

## The readers of a device (R-111)

Added 2026-10-27. How many readers hash a device's files follows whether it is rotational (R-110: one for an HDD, and for a device whose type is unknown; `min(8, cores)` for an SSD). The platform reports the type (Windows `StorageDeviceSeekPenaltyProperty`, Linux `/sys/block/<dev>/queue/rotational`), and a USB bridge may report it wrongly or not at all. R-111 makes `io.rotational_override` (by serial, §B7.3) and `--io hdd|ssd` win over that; only `--io` was implemented, per command, and the GUI, which has no `--io`, had no way at all.

1. **Values.** `"io": {"rotational_override": {"<serial>": "hdd"}}` in the config file (R-82): each device serial names `"hdd"` (rotational) or `"ssd"`; `true` and `false` are read as `"hdd"` and `"ssd"`. Any other value, or an `io` or `rotational_override` that is no object, makes the config invalid (exit 11), as a malformed `policy` does: a typing mistake would otherwise go unseen while the device is read the wrong way. `config_to_json` keeps the file's own `io` as it is.
2. **Which device.** A device takes the override whose serial equals its own as identity matching compares serials (`serials_equal`: case, spaces and underscores aside, and byte-swapped pairs), the serial that `media list` shows. A device without a serial takes none. A volume on several devices is rotational when any of them is (as before).
3. **Precedence.** `--io` wins; then the override; then what the platform detected (`volume_rotational`, `core/hash/hash_pipeline.h`).
4. **Where.** Registration, FULL verification and relocation, on the command line and in the GUI (Register Archive, Verify Archive, and Resume in the Archive List, which takes from the config what the command line takes given no option).
5. Tests: the rule itself (precedence, serials as matching compares them, two devices); the config's values and its refusals; each engine read by several readers at once where the override makes its HDD an SSD; the Archive List's Resume carrying the override; on the command line `--io hdd`, `--io ssd`, a refused `--io` and a refused override. That the command line and the GUI hand the config's override to the engines is reviewed: readers are not seen from outside.
